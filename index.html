Modeling and analysis of time series data: Bitcoin price volatility

Faculty-mentored research with Dr. Edward Ionides, Associate Chair for Undergraduate Studies in Statistics. The goal: find the time-series model that best explains how Bitcoin's volatility behaves, so risk can be measured and forecast.

  • Nearly 11 years of daily closing prices from Yahoo Finance, September 17, 2014 to July 19, 2025, a stretch where Bitcoin went from about $500 to $117,000.
  • Fit six model families in R (ARIMA, ARMA, ARCH, GARCH, EGARCH, GJR-GARCH), plus a grid of more than 40 GARCH-family fits up to order (4,4).
  • EGARCH(1,1) gave the best fit among the (1,1) models and captured Bitcoin's volatility clustering.
RrugarchMATLABTime seriesHypothesis testingValue-at-Risk

How I approached it

  1. Make it stationaryRaw prices failed the ADF test (p > 0.05). Daily log returns passed it (p = 0.01).
  2. Baseline modelauto.arima chose ARIMA(2,0,0), but it assumes constant variance, and Bitcoin's clearly isn't.
  3. Diagnose volatilityReturns barely correlate with their past, but squared and absolute returns do. That's volatility clustering. Q-Q plots showed fat tails.
  4. Fit the GARCH familyStandard GARCH, EGARCH, and GJR-GARCH with an ARMA(1,1) mean, compared by AIC and log-likelihood.
  5. Stress-test the winnerEGARCH passed the squared-residual Ljung-Box (p = 0.61) and ARCH-LM (p = 0.22) tests. Standard GARCH failed the Nyblom stability test.
What I found

Bitcoin's volatility is highly persistent (β ≈ 0.93): a shock keeps echoing for weeks. That makes GARCH-style models a natural fit, and I used the fitted volatility to draw 1% Value-at-Risk limits.

What the models missed

The ARMA(1,1) mean left some autocorrelation in the residuals, and normal errors understate how often extreme days happen.

What I'd do next

Refit with a heavy-tailed error distribution and a richer mean model, then compare out-of-sample volatility forecasts.

How LoRA works

Freeze the pretrained weights W and learn the update as ΔW = BA, two low-rank matrices. B starts at zero, so training begins exactly at the original model. Adapters can be swapped per task or merged back in.

What QLoRA adds

4-bit NormalFloat (NF4) puts quantization levels at the quantiles of a normal distribution. Double quantization cuts overhead from 0.5 to 0.127 bits per parameter. Paged optimizers prevent out-of-memory crashes.

Why it matters

The QLoRA paper's Guanaco model reached 99.3% of ChatGPT's performance on the Vicuna benchmark after 24 hours on one GPU. Universities and small labs can now adapt models themselves.

Limits I covered: low rank struggles to inject brand-new knowledge, 4-bit noise can hurt precision-heavy tasks, and fine-tuning cost isn't serving cost. Newer methods like DoRA and AdaLoRA try to close the gap with full fine-tuning.
